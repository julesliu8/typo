Conceptualising love in Amharic: Lexis, 
metaphors, and cultural contexts
Abstract
Based on three sets of linguistic data and grounded in cognitive ethnolinguistic method -
ology, this article analyses the Amharic concept of love at three levels of granularity. First, 
it examines the difference between the two verbal exponents of the concept wäddädä ‘to 
love, like, want’ and afäqqärä ‘to love’, along with the specifics of the noun fəqər ‘love’, as 
well as the multitude of other categories formed from the two main roots fqr and wdd. 
Second, it discusses the basic metaphors of love, with particular focus on those unique 
to Amharic (e.g. fəqər lay mähon lit. ‘to be on love’ , yäanǧät fəqər lit. ‘the intestine love’). 
Third, the article explores the most important subcategories of Amharic love, such as 
romantic love, family love, self-love and love of homeland. Overall, the article offers an 
in-depth account of the structure of the concept and demonstrates a range of links with 
fundamental beliefs and practices functioning in Ethiopian culture, such as the notion of 
an interventionist God, the central role of family relations, the tabooisation of sex, and 
a uniquely conceptualized relationship with one’s homeland. It also provides a strong 
argument for taking linguistic and cultural relativity into account in discussions of human 
experience and the conceptualization of love.
Keywords: concept of love, Amharic worldview, Amharic semantics, Ethiopian culture, 
ethnolinguistics

1. Introduction
Few notions have attracted the attention of scholars around the world as much 
as love. While most of the research in this area still treats it as a predominantly 
universal concept (Buss 1988; Bartels & Zeki 2000, 2004; Fisher et al. 2005; 
Shaver & Mikulincer 2006; Song et al. 2015), the past few decades have brought 
increased awareness of the key role that language and culture have on one’s 
perception of love (Kövecses 1986, 1991; Wierzbicka 1986, 1992, 2019; Beall 
& Sternberg 1995; Goddard 1995; Swidler 2001; Lomas 2018). Nevertheless, 
it is rather safe to say that the quest to uncover the different ways in which 
love is conceptualized and verbalized across the world’s languages is far from 
complete. The present study aims to contribute to this endeavour by providing 
a comprehensive account of the concept of love present in Amharic.
In many respects, the concept of love in Amharic presents a particularly inter -
esting case. On the one hand, it is strongly influenced by Christian philosophy, 
which has had a key role in shaping the concepts of love found in most European 
languages (Underhill 2012; Wierzbicka 2019). This means its scope and structure 
resemble those found in languages such as English or Polish, encompassing 
a great multitude of different domains of experience and subcategories (Bier -
wiaczonek 2002; Brożyna-Reczko 2017; Wierzbicka 2019). On the other hand, 
however, the specificity of the Amharic language and its role in shaping the 
Ethiopian culture have allowed it to develop a rather unique concept of love 
based upon this universal Christian platform. This is reflected in the categories 
of Amharic, particularly in the two main verbal exponents of the concept, but also 
in a number of other words and phrases regarding love and the related emotions 
and attitudes. At a more fine-grained level, Ethiopia’s cultural uniqueness has 
also meant that each of the subcategories of love in Amharic differs from their 
counterparts in other languages, both in content and prominence. Drawing on 
three sets of linguistic data, this study aims to examine the concept of love in 
Amharic at three different levels:
(1) A nalyse the lexico-semantic field centred around the main roots connected with the 
concept of love, fqr and wdd;
(2) E xamine the basic metaphors of love present in the Amharic lexicon and how they 
manifest in actual language use;
(3) A nalyse the basic characteristics of the main subcategories (types) of love in Amharic.
Overall, the study aims to provide a multifaceted picture of the concept of love 
encapsulated in Amharic while also laying the groundwork for cross-linguistic

2. Material and method 
The study falls within the field of cognitive ethnolinguistics, which assumes that 
each language embodies a unique model of the world, providing speakers with 
a framework for organizing their experience of reality and communicating it to 
other members of their linguistic community (Bartmiński 2009; Underhill 2012; 
Głaz 2022). This model will hereafter be referred to as a worldview, understood 
after Bartmiński as a “language-entrenched interpretation of reality, which can 
be expressed in the form of judgements about the world, people, things, events” 
(Bartmiński 2009: 23). For a long time, worldview studies were mainly focused 
on the most petrified side of language – the language system. The last two 
decades, however, have seen an increased interest in corpus, text, and even 
survey data (Underhill 2012: 31-40; Bartmiński 2018: 10-11; Głaz 2022: 196-8). 
These complementary data sources account for the open and dynamic nature 
of lexical categories, which are often broad enough to encompass different 
perspectives, thereby bringing the analysis closer to the people who use them. 
Given its scope and complexity, including additional data seems particularly 
crucial in the context of the concept of love in Amharic. Equally importantly, it 
also allows one to better capture the intertwining of the Amharic language and 
the unique Ethiopian culture reflected in it. Therefore, the present study employs 
three different types of linguistic data:
 • Lexicological data: Basic linguistic facts (lexicological definitions, main deriva-
tives and phrases) concerning the lexical representations of the concept were 
derived from the contemporary dictionary of Amharic Amarəñña mäzgäbä 
qalat (2001, henceforth AMQ), as well as from the older, but not rarely more 
comprehensive dictionary Addis yäʾamarəñña mäzgäbä qalat written by Desta 
Tekle Wolde (1970). In order to know the etymology of the chosen lexemes, 
two dictionaries of Ge’ez were used: Kidane Wold Kifle’s Mäṣhafä säwas əw 
wägəs wämäzgäbä qalat haddis (1956), as well as Wolf Leslau’s Comparative 
Dictionary of Ge’ez (1987). Finally, the lexicological data were supplemented 
by the idiom dictionary Yäamarəñña fäliṭočč (Amsalu & Daniachew 1983) and 
Thomas Leiper Kane’s Amharic-English Dictionary  (1990), which is particularly 
rich in phraseology.

3. Love in the Amharic lexicon4
In Amharic, there are two main roots connected with love – fqr and wdd – both 
with a long history dating back to the Ge’ez language. 5 Based on these two 
roots, one can distinguish three main lexical representations of the concept of 
love: the noun fəqər, as well as the verbs afäqqärä and wäddädä. When it comes 
to the meaning and scope of these categories, the simplest case is the nominal 
representation of the concept, which, for the most part, resembles the English 
lexeme love, as seen in the definition provided by AMQ:
1. ከፍተኛ የሆነ የመውደድ ስሜት (ለምሳሌ የሃገር ፍቅር /የእናት ፍቅር/ መንፈሳዊ ፍቅር)። 2. በሁለት ተቃራኒ 
ጾታዎች መካከል ያለና በወሲብ ፍላጎት ላይ የተመሰረተ የመዋደድ፣ የመሳሰብ ስሜት። 3. ወዳጅ፣ ስምም። 
እገሌ ና እገሌ ፍቅር ናቸው። ‘1. a strong feeling of loving [ mäwdäd] (e.g. love of homeland / 
motherly love / spiritual love) 2. a feeling of mutual loving [ mäwadäd] and care based 
on sexual desire linking two people of opposite sex 3. beloved, name. X and Y are very 
friendly / all love’, [lit. they are fəqər]’
When considering the first two senses in this definition, we see that the noun in 
question, just like its English equivalent, covers a wide range of relationships – 
from romantic love and motherly love to love of homeland and spiritual love. 
Regarding the structure of the definition itself, an interesting aspect is the decision 
to separate love between a man and a woman from other types of love, and to 
base this distinction on the aspect of sexual desire – an element that is not 
typically prominent in Amharic discourse and is considered somewhat taboo. 
This likely reflects the dictionary makers’ attempt to give the definition a more 
scientific character, especially given that this distinction is absent in the older 
dictionary by Desta. 6 Another noteworthy aspect is that the first two senses are

desire (non-sexually)’ and ‘to like’. The differences between the two verbs become

the difference is reflected in frequency: in the amWaC corpus, afäqqärä occurs 
only 323 times, while wäddädä appears 10,156 times, indicating that wäddädä 
serves as the default verb in most contexts. 
Apart from the main lexical representations of the concept, the existence of two 
roots connected with love, combined with the richness of Amharic morphology, 
offers extensive possibilities for creating derivatives that frame the concept from 
different angles. With verbal derivatives, adding appropriate prefixes or modifying 
the root regularly produces reciprocity ( täfäqaqqärä ‘to love each other, mostly 
in romantic love’, täwaddädä ‘to love each other (any relation), also to fit each 
other’), passivity ( täfäqqärä ‘be loved, mostly in romantic love’, täwäddädä ‘be 
loved, liked, popular, also to become more expensive’), and agency ( affäqqärä 
‘to make someone be loved (romantic love)’, affäqaqqärä ‘to make two people 
(romantic love) love each other’, asfäqqärä ‘to make someone be loved, compel 
to love someone’, awaddädä ‘to make people love each other, also raise price, 
make elements fit’, aswäddädä ‘to make something to be loved, also make the 
price rise’). The range of possibilities is even greater with derived nouns and 
adjectives. Among nouns based on the root wdd, the most frequent are wäddaǧ 
‘friend, beloved’10 and wäddaǧənnät ‘friendship’, which demonstrate the close ties 
between love and friendship. Other derivatives recorded in dictionaries – such 
as mäwwädäǧa ‘reason one is loved’ or ‘peaceful approach’ and awwädadäd 
‘way of loving’ – are less common in everyday use but nonetheless highlight 
the richness of the Amharic love lexicon.

wäddad, which serves as the base for a number of idioms denoting various 
attitudes such as rasun / ras wäddad  ‘egoistic, selfish’, säw wäddad ‘friendly’, 
or agär wäddad ‘patriotic’. This richness of the Amharic love-lexicon continues

4. Metaphors of love in Amharic
When it comes to the basic metaphoric properties of the Amharic concept of 
love, most of the patterns listed below should be familiar to the English-speaking 
audience of this article. To start with, the most fundamental metaphor of love in 
Amharic is undoubtedly the one that sees God as the ultimate source of love. 
Here, following the teachings of the Bible, God is perceived as love itself and 
the source from which all love comes . As for the ways in which this perfect 
and everlasting love of God is manifested in the human world, the above example 
demonstrates that it can be conceptualized as a container in which one lives (see 
the commonly used phrase ṭəlq fəqər ‘deep love’). This can also pertain to other 
types of love, such as love of a neighbour and romantic love, and, as illustrated 
in the examples below, the metaphor can be elaborated in a number of ways:
1. bäwəb yäfəqər aläm wəsṭ yalä hulättu lela f əṭur yallä aymäslaččäw əm näbbär። (Bealu

The other key metaphor is the love is an object metaphor. Following the logic 
of this metaphor, love can possess all the fundamental properties of a physical 
object: it can have a certain size ( käff yalä / t əlləq f əqər ‘great / big love’), be 
possessed (fəqər alläw ‘he has love’), be sought and found (fəqər fällägä / agäññä 
‘he sought / found love’), and be given and received (fəqər säṭṭä / täqäbbälä ‘he 
gave / received love’). Rather unsurprisingly, the feeling of love in Amharic can 
also be conceptualized in terms of fire ( bäfəqər täqaṭṭälä ‘he burned with love’, 
yäfəqər əsat ‘the fire of love’, yämmiyaqqaṭṭəl fəqər ‘burning love’), although this 
metaphor appears to be used primarily for one specific subcategory (romantic 
love), and within a specific discourse (songs and literature). What all these three 
metaphors (fluid, object, fire) have in common, however, is that they primarily 
represent love within the domain of feelings and emotions. When love is viewed as 
a mutual relationship, in turn, a different metaphor emerges – love is closeness.

Here, drawing from the fundamental human experience of physical closeness 
(see Kövecses 1986: 65-66), Amharic expresses love’s growth as two people 
coming closer ( täqärarräbu ‘they got closer to each other’ , qərb naččäw ‘they 
are close to each other’), while its decline is framed as increasing distance 
(täraraqən ‘we got further away from each other’). When seen from a long-time 
perspective, a love relationship can also be seen as a journey, with the lovers 
being conceptualized as travellers walking together through life’s ups and downs 
(yäfəqər guzo ‘the journey of love’). Nevertheless, within the source-path-goal 
image schema (Lakoff 1987: 286), more common in Amharic discourse is the 
perception of love in terms of a path on which one walks (yäfəqər mängäd mäg-
waz ‘to walk the path of love’), which is deeply connected to Christian ideology 
and the Christian attitude, which holds great significance in Ethiopia. Another 
common metaphor with strong Christian undertones portrays love as a ruler  
or conqueror (fəqər säffänä / ašännäfä ‘love reigned / pervaded / won’), to whom

core, is similar to those known from the European languages, including English.

mähon lit. ‘to be on love’, which is used to denote a state which one in English 
would call being in love. This expression is part of a broader pattern in Amharic,

5. The subcategories of Amharic love
As already observed, just like in other languages where the concept of love has 
been influenced by Christian philosophy (Underhill 2012; Wierzbicka 2019), love 
in Amharic is a broad concept encompassing a number of subcategories. Each 
of these subcategories is built around a distinct scenario and highlights differ -
ent aspects of the concept as a whole (Kövecses 1986; Bierwiaczonek 2002; 
Kacprzak 2022, 2025). The discussion of the subcategories of love in Amharic 
below is divided into four sections, which subsequently address romantic love, 
subcategories related to the family domain, religiously oriented subcategories, 
and non-prototypical subcategories. 
5.1. Romantic love
The prototypical status of romantic love in Amharic is evidenced by the fact that it 
functions as the default reference of a number of common idiomatic expressions 
involving the noun fəqər, such as the already mentioned fəqər yazäw ‘love seized 
him’ and bäfəqər wäddäqä ‘he fell in love’, which normally do not refer to any

5.2. Subcategories of love related to the family domain
When it comes to love within the family, the first observation is that it is usu -
ally only the less emotion-laden verb wäddädä that is used in this context, as 
afäqqärä would generally create an awkward impression when referring to any 
of its types. 17 This, however , does not mean that family love (yäbetäsäb f əqər) 
is perceived as less important within the Amharic worldview. Among all family 
relationships, particularly prominent in the context of love is the linguistically en -
trenched category yäənnat fəqər ‘motherly love’. Although it is considered one of 
the most universal forms of love – so much so that some anthropologists claim it 
to be the source of all other forms (see Shaver & Mikulincer 2006; Clark & Monin 
2006) – it is also shaped by cultural norms and beliefs (Johnston and Swanson 
2003: 21; Mary et al. 2024), which is well visible in the collected material. Here, 
still dominating is the ideal of an ever-sacrificing and devoted mother for whom 
motherhood is the ultimate fulfilment of her life’s purpose. Central to this image 
are the categories of mäswa’ətənnät ‘sacrifice’ and ənkəbkabe ‘care’. The other 
set of aspects commonly associated with motherly love involves affection and 
tenderness, not rarely combined with concern. Fatherly love, on the other hand, 
has traditionally been understood through the lens of the father as a strong 
leader and provider for the family, with affection and tenderness playing a less 
prominent role than in motherly love. 18 However, in contemporary Amharic liter -

‘patriot, lit. country-loving’, yagär f əqər ‘patriotism, lit. love of country’, but also 
ityop̣ p̣ əya ‘Ethiopia’, ityop̣ p̣ əyawinnät ‘ethiopianness’, and habäša ‘habesha’, 
which accumulate all the unique cultural and social traits for which Ethiopians 
love their country (Kacprzak, in press). 
Even more telling is the fact that love of Ethiopia is perhaps the only subcat -
egory, apart from romantic love, in which the verb afäqqärä is regularly used, 
highlighting one’s emotional engagement and readiness to sacrifice for the 
country. On a discursive level, the prominence of love for Ethiopia in popular 
music is particularly notable, with “Ethiopia is the mother of all Ethiopians” (ənnat 
agär lit. ‘mother land’, yähullaččənnəmm ənnat ‘the mother of us all’) being by 
far the most basic and common metaphor. Beyond language, this special love 
is also evident in the way Ethiopians incorporate their flag into their clothing, 
their pride in Ethiopia’s cultural heritage, and their inability to part with their 
traditional food, even when abroad (Kacprzak, in press). Finally, the concept 
of friendship in Amharic differs from that in other Christian-rooted languages, 
European languages in particular. Is friendship love? In Amharic, this question 
makes little sense. One cannot mafqär a friend – this would immediately shift 
the relationship into the sphere of romantic love. However, it is entirely normal 
to mäwdäd one’s friends – one’s wädaǧočč ‘friends’,22 a noun derived from the 
verb wäddädä. Likewise, referring to friendship in terms of the fəqər of friends 
is natural, as seen in a literary excerpt from the self-collected corpus, where the 
metaphoric phrase yäwändəmmamačč fəqər ‘love of brothers’ is used to describe 
two close friends. The ways of expressing friendship also differ. Grammatically, 
it is common to use feminine forms when addressing male friends, a practice 
intended to convey closeness and affection (Wołk-Sore 2008: 89-90, 2009: 131-
132). On a supralinguistic level, the physical closeness between male friends

6. Conclusions
Drawing on three sets of linguistic data, the above study examined the concept 
of love in Amharic at three different levels. First, it localized the lexical rep -
resentations of the concept, including the two verbs wäddädä and afäqqärä, the 
noun fəqər, and the many other categories derived from the two main roots fqr 
and wdd. Second, it identified the linguistically entrenched metaphors of love. 
While the vast majority of these metaphors are known from analyses of love 
metaphors in other languages, the data also included several expressions that 
appear to be more unique to Amharic, e.g. fəqər lay mähon ‘to be in love, lit. to 
be on love’ , yäan ǧät f əqər ‘true love, lit. the intestine love’ , and mäṭbäs ‘to hit 
on someone, lit. to fry’. The third part of the study analysed the various subcat -
egories encompassed by the concept, highlighting their linguistic and cultural 
specificity, and their ties with the rest of the Amharic worldview. Overall, love in 
Amharic emerges as a particularly broad concept profiled by a variety of different 
lexemes, understood via numerous metaphoric patterns, and encompassing 
a wide array of subcategories and related concepts.
The results of the above study do not necessarily compel one to automatically 
abandon the notion that, at the most basic level, love is something shared by all 
humans. In a slightly narrower context, despite highlighting a number of significant 
differences, the analysis has also demonstrated that the Amharic concept of love 
offers a large number of basic similarities with the corresponding concepts known 
from other languages of Christian heritage (Wierzbicka 2019; Kacprzak 2022). 
Nevertheless, the study clearly shows that the language- and culture-relative 
aspects of love should be taken as seriously as the universal ones (Kövecses 
1991; Wierzbicka 1992; Kacprzak 2022). This pertains not only to the most im -
mediate lexical representations of the concept. Throughout the analysis, we have 
seen that the concept of love in Amharic cannot be separated from other parts 
of the language and the worldview embedded within it – the basic metaphysical 
assumptions (such as the interventional God as the uncontested centre of the 
world), social values (including religiousness, gender roles, and the tabooisation 
of desire and sex), other lexical categories (such as those denoting emotional 
attitudes), and even the specifics of the morphological system (the prefixation 
and reduplication system, basic prepositions). This is what language and lexicon

are – an ecosystem interconnected on many levels, tied to the culture in which 
they have developed – a truth that must be remembered when analysing linguistic 
concepts and comparing them across the world’s languages (Underhill 2012; 
Kacprzak 2024b: 117). Additionally, significant differences may not only manifest 
in lexical facts but also in the prominence of certain phenomena, from single 
aspects and metaphoric patterns to entire subcategories. There is probably no 
better example of this than the religious subcategories and religiously-tinged 
metaphors of love in Amharic (not least the “God is love” metaphor), which, 
naturally, resemble those found in other Christian-rooted languages. However, 
their status is much more prominent in Amharic than, for instance, in Danish, 
which represents a strongly secular Western culture (Kacprzak 2022). On the 
contrary, the aspects of desire and sex, highly prominent in Western cultures 
(Underhill 2012: 96-106), are much less visible within the Amharic discourse on 
romantic love. 
In conclusion, the present study should be considered as another step towards 
understanding the concept of love present in Amharic, providing an in-depth 
account of the main factors that contribute to its specificity. However, the quest 
to uncover the uniqueness of the Amharic concept of love is far from over. To 
fully understand its specificity, this study should be followed by more detailed 
and fine-grained analyses of the subcategories of love in Amharic, and the 
discourses shaped around them, as well as the related concepts denoting 
emotions and emotional attitudes. Additionally, it is important to note that this 
study approached the Amharic concept of love primarily from the perspective 
of a common lexical platform through which Amharic speakers can categorise 
and communicate their experiences related to love. However, given Amharic’s 
status as both a lingua franca and an ethnic language, coupled with the diversity 
within Ethiopia (especially the gap between large cities and the countryside), it 
is spoken by a particularly diverse group of speakers. Therefore, incorporating 
additional data, such as questionnaires, to uncover the sociolinguistic differences 
in how Amharic speakers conceptualise love should be considered the next 
logical step following this study. Finally, to better highlight the specificity of the 
concept of love in Amharic and to ground the study more firmly in the African 
context, comparative studies involving other Ethiopian and African languages 
would be necessary.
