How universal is wanting? *
Olesya Khanina
Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology
he paper reports on a cross-linguistic survey of translational equivalents of 
the Standard Average European concept of wanting. It is conducted on a variety 
sample of 73 languages, each of which was checked for morphosyntactic and se-
mantic properties of its regular means for expressing wanting, i.e. desideratives. 
Desideratives are shown to typically have other meanings in addition to ‘want’ , 
an array of modal and mental-emotive senses. I suggest that the combination 
of these meanings with ‘want’ can insightfully be analyzed as a macrofunction, 
each language making its own decision about the set of situations in which its 
desideratives can be used. he oten-claimed status of W ANT as an alleged uni-
versal semantic prime is thus seriously called into question by the evidence from 
languages which always express wanting together with some other situations.
Keywords: semantics, typological sample, macrofunctionality, semantic prime; 
wanting, desiderative, modal semantic domain, mental-emotive semantic 
domain
1. Introduction
1.1 Setting the problem
How is the idea of wanting coded by the languages of the world? Can one spea k 
of any uniformity or, at least, universal principles? For a European, wanting seems 
to be a cultural prime, something self-evident and in no need of explanation. 
hus, ‘I want / He wants’ easily translates from one European language to anoth-
er.1 he same logic underlies the Eurocentric conception of W ANT as a lexical or 
even semantic universal, i.e. a concept inding (lexical) expression in any human 
language.
But is this true? To answer the question I checked possible ways to express 
the ‘Standard Average European’ situation of wanting in 73 languages making up 
a representative typological sample. he point of departure for this quest was t he 
set of desiderative verbs of English, German, French, Spanish and Russian, i.e. the

practical meta-languages of most descriptive grammars (cf. want/would like, wol-
len/mögen, vouloir/désirer, querer, xotet’/xotet’sja). Having enumerated the struc-
tures used in the sample languages to render the meaning of these verbs, I pro-
ceeded to make generalizations about how uniform such structures are and how 
well-proiled and salient the idea of wanting is.
1.2 Methodology
For this study a variety sample was used. he sampling method was that proposed 
in Rijkhof & Bakker (1998), which is based on the idea that the genealogical tree 
of a language family relects the degree of linguistic diversity within the family. 
hus, the more branching nodes there are in the tree of a given family, the higher 
the diversity value of the family is. he higher the diversity value of the family, in 
turn, the more languages were included in the sample from the family.
A predeined list of families for a 100-languages sample was taken from Ri-
jkhof & Bakker (1998), though it was slightly modiied in light of recent clas-
siication improvements (cf. Gordon 2005) and the poor documentation of some 
language families. For the current sample, from each family the language wit h 
the highest level of documentation was chosen (in line with standard typologi-
cal practice). Missing languages, i.e. inadequately described languages from un-
derdocumented language families, were not replaced by languages from better-
documented families, in order to avoid distortion of the initial balance. hus the 
inal size of my sample was 73 languages, belonging to 63 language families. See 
Appendix 1 for the full list of languages of the sample.
he main sources of information about wanting were descriptive grammars, 
bilingual dictionaries, and translated, and oten glossed, text collections;2 informa-
tion on 19 languages was complemented by detailed personal consultations wit h 
specialists in these languages.3

2.2 Factors conditioning the distribution of multiple meanings
When the same language expression can have two (or more) meanings, the ques-
tion of the distribution of these multiple meanings arises. here are three logical 
possibilities:
a. there exists some morphosyntactic/semantic factor which conditions the dis-
tribution, and
 a1.  each value of this factor allows only one of the two meanings (i.e. ‘fully 
conditioned distribution of meanings’);
[PDF PAGE 6]
© 2008. All rights reserved
822 Olesya Khanina
 or
 a2.  some values of this factor allow both meanings, whereas some allow only 
one of them (i.e. ‘partially conditioned distribution of meanings’);
b. there is no morphosyntactic/semantic factor at all conditioning the distrib u-
tion, i.e. only the general pragmatic or discourse context may cause a meaning 
preference; taken out of context, both meanings are always possible.
In actual practice, of course, there always is the fourth possibility of no info rma-
tion available about the distribution of the meanings. Moreover, in a situ ation of 
lack of direct access to speakers of most of the sample languages, there oten is 
hardly any evidence for possibility (b), which is then indistinguishable from the 
fourth possibility.

7. Discussion: Is wanting universal?
In the previous section I have shown that the structures used in the w orld’s lan-
guages to render the ‘European’ situation of wanting tend to have more than one 
translational equivalent in English and other European languages. How can t his 
situation be analyzed and what lies behind the translational equivalents?
Probably the classical answer to the question would be to invoke polysemy , 
thus admitting polysemy to be common for desideratives. Such a point of view 
is expressed in Harkins (1995), 23 a study of wanting in the framework of Anna 
Wierzbicka’s Natural Semantic Metalanguage (NSM): cf. the author’s remark “It 
has been observed that the commonest elements in languages are those most likely 
to be polysemous” (Harkins 1995: 234–235). he next analytical step would then 
be a search for the conditions on such a polysemy, i.e. “speciiable diferences in 
syntactic or semantic contexts of occurrence” (Harkins: ibid.). As Harkins’ study 
acknowledges and makes use of the polysemy analysis, it is worth looking at an 
example of how the conditions are stated there. Guided by her concept of “disam-
biguation strategies” , the author comments on the polysemy of the Bahasa Indone-
sia desiderative suka in (28a–b):

She says:
…it does not mean that in Bahasa Indonesia the concepts ‘want’ and ‘like’ are 
somehow merged. When suka is used in its desiderative sense, mau ‘want’ can be 
substituted for it; and when suka refers to liking, mau cannot be substituted for 
it… (Harkins 1995: 58)
In other words, the polysemy is admitted to be conditioned if at least one of the 
meanings of a polysemous linguistic expression can be substituted in all its usages 
by a semantically equivalent linguistic expression, while the second meaning can-
not be substituted by the same expression without semantic change.
By itself, this argument is lawed, as it potentially gives rise to an ininite num-
ber of so-called “polysemies” . For example, using the same logic, one could argue 
that the English verb to want is polysemous between ‘want very much’ and ‘want 
a little bit’ , as only the irst meaning, but not the second one, can be substituted by 
the English verb to long for.
his last example suggests a diferent approach. When an English speaker uses 
the verb to want, (s)he does not need to specify whether ‘want very much’ or ‘want 
a little bit’ is meant, even though (s)he can, if necessary. Similarly, a Bahasa Indo-
nesia speaker using suka might not need to specify whether ‘want’ or ‘like/love’ is 
meant, even though (s)he also can, if necessary.
hese considerations evoke the notion of macrofunctionality, anticipated al -
ready in Comrie (1998) and Gil (2000), but distinctly formulated only in Gil (2004: 
372): “Unlike polyfunctionality, which presupposes a plurality of functions, mac-
rofunctionality assumes a single function, which may or may not be viably decom-
posable into a set of constituent subfunctions” . In other words, some lan guages 
make distinctions that are insigniicant for other languages, and in such cases there 
are generally no objective criteria to deine which group of languages do this more 
‘naturally’ and thus represent a more basic semantic pattern, and which do it less 
‘naturally’ and thus represent a derived semantic pattern.24 he simplest example 
might be that of color systems: e.g., English has one word blue whereas Russian has 
two words, sinij ‘dark blue’ and goluboj ‘light blue’ . he notion of macrofunctional-
ity enables one to say that neither the English nor the Russian pattern i s “basic” , 
and that the English blue is macrofunctional, unifying in one function the ideas of 
‘dark blue’ and ‘light blue’ . In fact, what is perceived as diferent functions by one 
language (e.g., ‘light blue’ and ‘dark blue’ by Russian, or a situation of wanting and 
a situation of liking by English) can readily be perceived as a single function by  
[PDF PAGE 27]
© 2008. All rights reserved

another language (e.g., ‘blue’ by English, or a situation of wanting/liking by Bahasa 
Indonesia). here is no way to tell whether the irst language is splitting up o ne 
“originally” unitary semantic unit, or whether the second language is unifying two 
“originally” distinct units.25 And in a situation of lack of evidence for either hy -
pothesis it seems more objective not to accept the irst one just because European 
languages thereby emerge as ‘more natural’ , but to formulate a new and distinct 
hypothesis whereby the diferences between the two former hypotheses are es -
sentially neutralized. ‘Macrofunctionality’ looks like such a hypothesis — a weaker 
one, but one closer to the facts: it suggests that we think in terms of ‘semantic 
vagueness’ instead of ‘semantic ambiguity’ when approaching linguistic items of 
the type of English blue vis-à-vis items of the type of Russian sinij and goluboj.
his is the approach I propose to take here for desideratives. Some desider-
atives are macrofunctional, while others are not: the so-called ‘multiple meanings’ 
are thus reinterpreted as macrofunction. he results of my study make it rea son-
able to posit that the linguistic objects called desideratives are used to denote a 
spectrum of physical and mental states. he spectral width can vary from quit e 
narrow, comprising just wanting, to considerably broader, comprising other situa-
tions enumerated in Sections 3–4 as well. It is, though, crucial to claim that — un-
til the opposite is proven — the spectrum is perceived as indivisible in each case: 
even speakers of languages where it is quite wide nonetheless perceive it as a uni-
tary whole. An additional argument for the claim is the preference (mentioned in 
Section 5) that languages have for desideratives with only one meaning in addition 
to ‘want’; arguably, more complex ‘combinations of meanings’ are more diicult to 
perceive as an indivisible whole.
Having chosen this type of reasoning, I ind myself quite skeptical regarding 
the following relection of Harkins (1995: 59) re Bahasa Indonesia suka:
We may like cofee but not want any just now; or we may ind that when we g et 
something we want, we don’t like it ater all.
his idea of wanting vs. liking is based on the English lexicon, which makes a clear 
distinction between wanting and liking. I have serious doubts whether the relation-
ship between the two situations is the same in a language having one word suka for 
both of them. Moreover, the result of language-culture interaction is evident here: 
it is diicult, if indeed possible, to determine whether speakers of Bahasa Indone-
sia have one word where English speakers have two words because of their culture, 
or whether their culture does not diferentiate between the two situations because 
of their language. Similarly, the inventory of meanings/functions available to the 
desideratives of the world languages, as presented in this paper, has been based on 
the European cultural model and perforce contains only those situations that are 
conceivable for speakers of a Standard Average European language.

Following the same logic, the label ‘desideratives’ is worth using only as a start-
ing point of investigation or as a useful methodological heuristic, while it might 
not be a pertinent reality in the languages of the world. Actually, as many as 30 lan-
guages of the representative sample of 73 languages have only desideratives which 
denote a situation of wanting and also some other (non-wanting) situation — i.e. 
in these languages, expressing the situation of “wanting” (in European terms) is 
oten impossible without simultaneously expressing some other situation.
herefore, the status of W ANT as a semantic prime/universal, as claimed in the 
Natural Semantic Metalanguage framework, is not evident. While its proponents do 
acknowledge the problem (Harkins 1995, Goddard & Wierzbicka 1994, Goddard & 
Wierzbicka 2002), they insist on the existence of syntactic or semantic conditions 
unambiguously governing the choice between W ANT and the other meaning in al-
most every case of a desiderative having an alternative meaning.26 But in Sections 3 
and 4 of this paper it was shown that a fully conditioned distribution of meanings 
is really quite rare (only 27 meanings out of 162, i.e. 17%) and that a partially con-
ditioned distribution is only about twice as frequent (44 meanings out of 162, i .e. 
27%); a conditioned distribution of any kind is thus attested in only 44% of cases.27
However, the methodology of the polysemy issue is far from being very trans-
parent in the NSM framework on itself, in a sense that an outsider cannot always 
prove or disprove its existence for a given lexeme, cf. “ …there seem to be no rig-
orous external (= external to the NSM theory assumptions, O.Kh.) controls on the 
postulation of polysemy” (Riemer 2003: 291).28 In Koptjevskaja-Tamm & Ahlgren 
(2003: 254–259) it was rather convincingly demonstrated that the NSM theory is 
indeed highly speculative re the distinction between polysemy and allolexy, and in 
passim, between vagueness and polysemy: “ …determining whether we are dealing 
with allolexy or with polysemy can be far from obvious — and that diferences and 
similarity may be (and oten are) estimated relatively to the predetermined goal” 
(ibid: 258). herefore I suspect that NSM theorists might ind some arguments 
to disprove my statement based on the absence of any conditions governing t he 
polysemy of desideratives in so many languages, using exactly the same data. It 
means, however, that it is not possible to determine the status of the phen omena 
(vagueness vs. polysemy) in the framework of the NSM theory, because, as stated 
above, this theory gives the ‘predetermined goal’ the power to decide it.
Does this mean that my reasoning in this section is pointless? Not necessar-
ily, as I assume the readers might accept not only argumentation in lines with the 
NSM theory, but also beyond it. hus, my claim that ‘want’ is not a semantic prime 
might not really hold, as this claim is (or is not) valid only within the theory of se-
mantic primes, i.e. the NSM theory. However it can be reformulated in a way free 
from any references to the NSM: ‘want’ does not represent a semantic unit inding 
its exclusive expression in all languages of the world.

hus my survey of desideratives questions the W ANT universal, not primarily 
on the basis of theoretical reasons, though these do exist, but rather by ap peal to 
new evidence difering crucially from what was known about desideratives earlier. 
Relecting on the sources of the diference, it could have emerged either from my 
having used a more principled sample,29 or from the absence of any preconceived 
idea of the results. In planning and conceptualizing my study, I did not aim to 
prove or disprove the existence of a W ANT universal, but just to examine what 
wanting looks like cross-linguistically.
My results suggest that there are languages where W ANT is indeed an indivis-
ible and indeinable salient concept of the culture; and there are languages where 
it is not salient, being treated only as particular type of a more general si tuation. 
To put it diferently, the salience of wanting is not universal, but rather a 
parameter of cross-linguistic variation.
he idea of the parametric nature of the lexicalization of the NSM semantic 
primes is not new. Earlier Bohnemeyer (2003: 219–220) suggested such an option 
for any possible prime: “ …what if semantic primes are like phonological features, 
in that there is a universal superset of which diferent languages may select difer-
ent coherent subsets?” In other words, the author suggests to get rid of the Strong 
Lexicalization Hypothesis and start a scientiic program which “explores the condi-
tions under which particular meanings are lexicalized across languages” . See also a 
similar statement pronounced by an author of the NSM volume Goddard & Wierz-
bicka (1994): “I doubt that all languages ‘have words for’ the conceptual primitives. 
his in no way challenges the idea of conceptual primitives, since concepts do not 
have to have names to be real” (Hale 1994: 282). hus, following these authors, who 
seem to be sympathetic with the idea of existence of semantic primitive units, but 
not with that of their universal lexicalization, the present paper has suggested some 
empirical data supporting non-universality of the ‘want’ lexicalization.
However there still persists the question of why some desideratives denote a 
wider spectrum of situations, while others denote a narrower, more European-like 
one — i.e. what conditions the value of the parameter of saliency of ‘want’ (or , 
lexicalization of ‘want’ in terms of the NSM)? here are two possible modes of ex-
planation here: with reference either to language-internal or to language-external 
factors. In the former case, the structural properties of a given language determine 
the functional range of its desideratives; in the latter case, something beyond t he 
language itself, e.g. some speciic facts of the culture, determines the function of de-
sideratives in the given language. While the irst approach seems more traditional 
for linguistics, I would cast doubt on it: the languages having ‘wider’ desideratives 
are too numerous, they belong to too many geographical areas and — by the nature 
of the sample — to too many genealogical families to have any common structural 
properties which are absent from languages with ‘narrow’ desideratives.

8. Conclusion
he main indings of the paper are:
1. Desideratives which are regularly used to express other situations as pure 
‘want’ are much more frequent in the languages of my sample than desider-
atives which can be used only to express wanting (95 to 41).
2. From desideratives having additional meanings the most frequent are with 
only one additional meaning, followed by desideratives with several additional 
meanings belonging, though, to the same semantic group (‘modal’ or ‘mental-
emotive’); summing up, the simpler the ‘addition’ to the ‘want’ meaning is, the 
more chances it has to be attested by a desiderative.
3. Desideratives of all morphosyntactic types (verbs, nouns, aixes and parti-
cles), except constructions, show roughly the same proportion of desideratives 
having other meaning in addition to ‘want’; yet each morphosyntactic type of 
desideratives favors only one semantic group as a source for the additional 
meanings: desiderative aixes and particles tend to have additional modal 
meanings, while desiderative verbs and nouns tend to have additional mental-
emotive meanings.
4. Additional meanings of desideratives can be divided into core and periph-
eral: the core meanings are generally more frequent and are also attested by 
desideratives whose morphosyntactic type does not favor the given semantic 
[PDF PAGE 32]
© 2008. All rights reserved
848 Olesya Khanina
type; the core modal meanings are ‘near future/prospective’ , ‘necessity’ , ‘inten-
tion’ , ‘obligation’ , ‘illocution/causation’ , ‘purpose’ , and ‘ability’ , the core mental-
emotive meanings are ‘like/love’ , ‘think’ , and ‘say’ .
5. Analysis of this cross-linguistic data has suggested that there is a signiicant 
variation in the spectrum of situations expressible by desideratives in the world 
languages, while the spectrum itself is claimed to be indivisible in individual 
languages. his claim is based on the fact that 30 from 73 sample languages 
have only such ‘broad’ desideratives which express more than a European situ-
ation of ‘wanting’ , and for all these desideratives there were found no regular 
syntactic or semantic factors conditioning what was traditionally analyzed as 
‘polysemy’ .
6. hus, the idea of the universal salience of wanting, which is based on Euro-
pean linguistic and cultural evidence, was shown to be erroneous. Slightly less 
than half of the sample languages cannot — and do not need to — express the 
unambiguous concept of wanting, at least in some grammatical contexts; i.e. 
in these contexts wanting is just an interpretation of a broader or more com-
plex concept.
More speciically, I have argued that ‘want’ is not a universal semantic pr ime in 
the sense of the Natural Semantic Metalanguage. here are too many languages all 
over the world that fail to express it through a distinct word, morpheme or ixed 
paraphrase, a necessary condition for a semantic prime Goddard (1994: 13). his 
very possibility of disproving a semantic prime is mentioned by Goddard (1998: 
152): he says that a good counter-argument to any prime would be to show that in 
some language “there is apparently no term whatever which can be used to convey 
unambiguously the same idea as expressed” by the supposed prime.
To conclude I would like to cite the following statement of an NSM theorist
his list ( =list of semantic primitives, O.Kh.) was not seen to be inal, and it was 
also clear that the correctness of such a list could not be proved in a positive sense. It 
could only be demonstrated to be false, in that it had to be tested by means of con-
crete semantic investigations (emphasis is mine, O.Kh.). (Durst 2003: 162)
I hope my semantic investigation has demonstrated convincingly enough that the 
inclusion of W ANT in this list is indeed false.32
