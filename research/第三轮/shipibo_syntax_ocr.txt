8.1. Alignment
8.1.1. Ergative-Absolutive Case-Marking System
In terms of alignment, SK exhibits a fairly rigid ergative-absolutive case-marking system.
Unlike what is most commonly found in languages of this type (Dixon 1994:83-104),
including those of the Panoan family, it can be said that in SK there are no instances of
case-marking splits triggered by the inherent semantics of the noun phrase, tense-aspect-
modality distinctions, or the syntactic status of the clause (but cf. case-marking
alternations due to the pragmatic status of the object in progressive and desiderative
constructions, in sections 7.12.2.1 and 8.2.8 respectively). The ergative case is marked
by the phrasal suffix-n. The absolutive marker is -a for the first singular, second
singular, and first plural personal pronouns, as well as for the human interrogative tso-

8.2.3. Transitive Clauses
8.2.3.1. Properties of Transitive Clauses
Transitive clauses are headed by inherent or derived transitive verbs. A transitive verb
exhibits the following properties: it requires its subject and object to be marked ergative
and absolutive, respectively; it imposes overt A agreement on locative / allative and
manner adverbials, and triggers zero participant agreement inflection on ablatives and
emphatic pronouns; it may take the dedicated malefactive applicative and the middle
suffix -t; it requires the plural-transitive forms of the suppletive andative and venitive
pairs; it combines with transitive forms of phasal verbs and may take the auxiliary ak-; it
may take the distributive -pake.
In SK, omission of required subject or object is normally understood as a zero
third person singular form, unless otherwise indicated by the context (Valenzuela 1997).

8.2.5. Clauses with Cognate Object Verbs
Clauses with cognate object verbs exhibit distinct degrees of intermediate transitivity
status. Cognate object verbs are those that take as their object a noun which does not
contribute to the meaning of the sentence. The overt expression of their object tends to
be more acceptable when these objects are somehow modified. In some of these cases,
the modifiers contribute to the meaning of the clause, functioning rather like an adverbial
(Levin 1993:95-6). The name "cognate object verbs" is due to the fact that the object is a
noun-form of the verb (and thus "cognate"), or of a semantically-related verb. Cross-
linguistically, clauses with cognate object verbs tend to conform to the agent-subject and
patient-object syntactic case-marking pattern, thus resembling a transitive clause (Givón
1984:105). Here the notion of "cognate object verb" has been extended to instances
where their object NPs are very restricted semantically and do not contribute to the
meaning of the sentence, even though the verb roots and their objects are not genetically
related. Some SK cognate object verbs are: bake-n- 'deliver (a baby),' bewa- 'sing,"

8.2.6. Clauses with Prefixed Verbs
SK has a set of prefixes that refer to parts of the body and are usually formed by taking
the first syllable of the correspondent nouns (sec 6.2.6). These body-part prefixes
productively combine with a set of intransitive and transitive verbs typically adding
adverbial information, especially a locative relationship, with respect to the particular
body-part; the meaning of the resulting stems is largely predictable. Hence body-part
prefixes do not occupy an argument slot and the resulting verb forms generally keep the
transitivity value of its base. Most clauses with prefixed verbs can be analyzed as
obligatory "possessor raising" constructions, where the possessor is coded as a core
argument while the possessed body-part functions as oblique.
There does not seem to exist a single criterion to distinguish verbs that take body-
part prefixes from those that do not. However, this class of verbs might be accounted for
by considering a cluster of meaning components, such as contact, motion / position,
causation, and (change of) state. Some verbs exhibit a shortened root form when
prefixed. Therefore, in these cases the verb stem is composed of a body-part prefix and a
reduced verb root. A list of body-part prefixes was provided in 6.2.6.
