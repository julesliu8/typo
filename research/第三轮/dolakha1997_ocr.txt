OBJECT RELATIONS AND DATIVE CASE IN DOLAKHA NEWARI
CAROL GENETTI
University of California, Santa Barbara
ABSTRACT
In Dolakha Newari, the dative case marks recipients of ditransitive verbs and some
patients of monotransitive verbs. Quantative studies show that animacy and activation
cost are both relevant in determining the distribution of dative case. Two theories on
the syntactic status of objects in systems of this general type are shown to be
inadequate for Dolakha. Instead it is found that there is no morphological or syntactic
evidence for differentiating between classes of object in this language. Theories on
the motivation of this pattern are considered, and it is concluded that it is best
understood in terms of referent importance or topicworthiness.

The factors which trigger the appearance of the dative casemarker on patients
are semantic and pragmatic in nature, as will be demonstrated below.
This type of patterning, in which some patients of monotransitives have
similar casemarking or other morphosyntactic behavior to recipients of
ditransitives, is well attested in the world's languages, as illustrated in cross-
linguistic studies dedicated specifically to this phenomenon by Blansitt
(1984), Dryer (1986), Noonan (1991), La Polla (1992) and Masica (1982);
Foley (1986: 101-102) also discusses this pattern in his description of the
languages of New Guinea. The primary issue to be addressed in these
languages is that of the syntactic status of patients of monotransitive verbs,
both with and without casemarking. Does the casemarking indicate that
patients of monotransitives and recipients hold a single grammatical relation
with the verb, and if so, what is it? In Dolakha, this discussion must also
include consideration of monotransitive patients which lack the dative
casemarker. Another, more general issue is of course why dative case should
be used to mark patients at all, in other words, what motivates this pattern.
This paper aims to address these two questions by providing an in depth
study of the phenomenon in Dolakha Newari. In particular, it includes a
quantitative study which explores the conditions under which the dative
casemarker appears on patients of monotransitive verbs in narrative text
(Section 2.0). Following this, it is argued that one cannot divide Dolakha
objects into two separate classes based on morphosyntactic behavior (3.0).

Instead it will be shown that patients and recipients together form a single
syntactic category of object, and that the assignment of casemarking in
Dolakha is independent of grammatical relations.
1.1 Theoretical Assumptions
This paper is written within a functional-typological framework. There
are two basic assumptions of this approach which are relevant to the current
study and which I therefore state explicitly. First, it is assumed that evidence
for syntactic categories is based upon language-particular morphosyntactic
behavior. Contrary to work in Relational Grammar and Lexical-Functional
Grammar, I do not assume that grammatical relations are primitive, nor do
I assume that they are configurationally defined, as in Government and
Binding Theory. Instead, I assume (following c.g. Keenan 1976, Comrie
1989, Borg and Comrie 1984, Gil 1984, Siewierska 1991, Mithun 1991, Van
Valin 1993a) that the degree to which a language can be said to exhibit, for
example, a subject, is dependent upon the degree to which the single direct
argument of an intransitive clause and the agentive argument of a transitive
clause have the same morphological and syntactic behavior. Thus some
languages such as English or Dolakha may have very strong subjects, some
languages such as Cayuga may have no subjects at all (Mithun 1991), and
some, such as Kathmandu Newari, may have only weak subjects, in that
there is only a small amount of behavior that characterizes the arguments
grouped as subjects.
A second assumption of most practitioners of functional-typological
theory is that there is only one level of syntactic structure, which is surface
structure (this assumption is often implicit, as in Givón 1979, 1984, Comrie
1989, Langacker 1988, Croft 1990, and is sometimes made explicit, e.g. Van
Valin 1993a). Distinctions between, for example, active and passive sentenc-
es, are not seen to be the result of movement rules which create a derived
from an underlying structure, but are instead analyzed as grammaticalized
constructions which reflect either historically or synchronically their basic
semantic, pragmatic and discourse functions. Thus the English passive is
accounted for not by a set of abstract rules relating levels of structure, but as
a grammaticalized construction available to English speakers which reflects
the function of the passive directly by placing the patient in the topical
position of subiect, and indicating the non-topical status of the agent by
either ellipsis or oblique status (Givón 1979, 1984).

Dolakha is a verb-final language. The order of the arguments preceding
the verb is flexible, with all permutations of arguments attested frequently.
The principles which govern the order of arguments have yet to be deter-
mined; they appear to be pragmatic in nature.

Although we have found a clear correlation between previous mention
and the presence of the casemarker, it should not be assumed that the dative
case is simply used to mark previous mention, or that speakers conduct some
sort of mental scan for earlier reference to a participant each time a human
patient is referred to. From what we understand about the complexities of
information flow in discourse, such an approach would clearly seem simplis-
tic. Instead, the current results may be interpreted in terms of current theories
of discourse and participant reference.
The distinction between previous mention and new is clearly reminis-
cent of the well known distinction between given and new (Chafe 1976),
which has now been expanded to include an intermediate category "accessi-
ble" (Chafe 1987, 1992). I will adopt Chafe's terminology in referring to
given, accessible and new as "activation costs". They may be defined as
follows:
(15) 3 Activation Costs (Chafe 1987, 1992)
Given concepts are those which the speaker considers to be active
for the hearer at the time of reference (in the hearer's current focus
of consciousness).
Accessible concepts are those which the speaker judges to be
semiactive for the hearer (in the hearer's peripheral consciousness).
New concepts are those which the speaker judges to be neither
given nor accessible.

Givenness can be established either through the extralinguistic context shared
by speaker and hearer, or the linguistic context of previous mention. Partici-
pants are accessible if they have been mentioned in the discourse, but have
moved out of focal consciousness due to the addition of more active partici-
pants or change in episodes or paragraph boundaries (Chafe 1987: 29).
Accessible participants may also be invoked via schema, a cluster of
interrelated concepts. For example, in a conversation about a basketball
game, the basketball, hoop, referee, shot clock, and other related concepts
come into peripheral consciousness automatically. New participants are those
not previously mentioned or invoked via schema.
If the dative case in Dolakha is coding activation cost as opposed to
simply noting whether or not a participant has been previously mentioned,
then there should be evidence for this in places where activation cost and
previous mention do not match up in a one-to-one manner. Such evidence is
found when we examine the counter-examples to the pattern in which
previously mentioned human patients are marked with dative case.
Three examples had human patients which had been previously men-
tioned in the narrative, but had not been referred to for significant lengths of
time (previous references were 105, 181, and 292 clauses back, respectively).
When they were reintroduced into the narrative in the patient role, these
carlier participants were not casemarked by =ta. If we were to assume that
the dative case simply marked previous reference, then these examples could
not be explained. Within the theory of activation cost, however, these
examples are accounted for, as the previous mentions were so much earlier
that the speakers could not assume that the hearers still retained these
characters even in peripheral consciousness. Hence the speakers chose to
reintroduce them as new. It might be noted in this regard that the plots of the
two narratives in which these three examples were found had numerous
twists and turns, and that the reintroductions of the participants were not
expected or foreshadowed. These facts strongly suggest that it is given or
accessible activation cost, as opposed to simple previous mention. that the
speaker is attending to.

2.4 Conclusions of the quantitative study
From the study of animacy and previous mention we can draw the
following conclusions. Inanimate NPs are never casemarked by =ta, animate
non-human NPs may be casemarked by =ta if given and crucial to the story
at the time of mention. Human NPs casemarked by =ta are either given or
accessible.
Animacy and activation cost are both factors that contribute to the
overall importance, or "topicworthiness" (S. Thompson 1990) of a referent.
Animate and in particular human referents are inherently more important
than inanimates (Ransom 1979, Givón 1984a,b) (all other things being equal;
the relative importance of specific human and inanimate referents may be
reversed in, certain discourse contexts). And arguments increase in topicality
(in the sense of Givón 1983) once they have been introduced, commented

upon, and integrated into the discourse. Thus while this study has examined
animacy and activation cost in particular, it may be seen as adding to the
growing body of evidence that referent importance or "topicworthiness" (S.
Thompson 1990) has a crucial role in determining morphosyntactic structure.
3. The syntactic status of patients and recipients in Dolakha
I will now turn to a discussion of the syntactic status of patients and
recipients in Dolakha. I will begin by discussing two earlier proposed
analyses of similar data, these being Dryer's (1986) theory of primary
objectivity, and Noonan's (1991) postulation of a rule of Antidative Shift. I
will demonstrate that both of these approaches are problematic for Dolakha.
I will then argue that patients and recipients together form a single syntactic
category "object" in Dolakha, and that there is not sufficient morphosyntactic
evidence to justify further division of the object category.
3.1 Primary objectivity
Dryer (1986) distinguishes between two types of languages in terms of
their treatment of objects. Primary object languages are those in which the
patient of the monotransitive predicate and the recipient of the ditransitive
predicate receive the same morphosyntactic treatment, hence form a syntactic
category "primary object" (PO). This contrasts with the category "secondary
object"' (SO), which includes patients of ditransitive predicates. Primary
object languages are distinguished from direct object languages. The latter
have the more traditionally recognized pattern in which patients of both
monotransitive and ditransitive verbs form a single syntactic category "direct
object" (DO) in contrast to recipients which fall into a separate category of
"indirect object" (IO). These two patterns may be represented as in Figures

It is clear that Dolakha does not neatly follow either of these patterns.
When patients are casemarked they are receiving the same treatment as
recipients of ditransitive verbs, hence for these cases Dolakha appears to be
a primary object language. Indeed, if one were to work only with limited
elicited data, one would probably reach this conclusion. On the other hand,
the numerous examples of non-casemarked patients in discourse show that in
terms of casemarking the patients of monotransitives and ditransitives are
being treated in the same manner, which would argue that Dolakha follows
the opposite pattern and is in fact a direct object language.
One possible avenue by which one might reconcile the data with the
theory is to analyze Dolakha casemarking as being a split system, in other
words, a system in which both primary and direct objectivity are attested. In
monotransitive predicates with patients which are human and given or
accessible, the patient has the same morphosyntactic behavior as recipients
of ditransitives, hence the primary object pattern. On the other hand, mono-
transitive patients which do not have these semantic and pragmatic properties
are given the same morphosyntactic treatment as ditransitive patients, hence
the direct object pattern is attested. On this analysis, Dolakha appears to
exhibit a split in the object marking pattern, a phenomenon referred to by
Dryer (1986: 827) as split objectivity. i3 This pattern would constitute a nice
parallel to split ergative casemarking systems which can also have semantic
motivations; for example, the Nepali ergative system is split by aspect.

while not stated explicitly, there is nothing in Dryer's framework which
excludes the possibility of a language which makes no distinction in object
categories (Matthew Dryer, personal communication). Dryer's framework
leaves open the possibility of a language in which all relevant rules operate
on the basis of a single category of object. Dolakha is an example of this
possibility.
3,2 Anti-Dative Shift
Noonan (1991), examining similar casemarking patterns in Chantyal
(also a Tibeto-Burman language of Nepal), proposes a quite different
analysis. He argues in favor of a rule of Anti-Dative Shift (ADS) (not to be
confused with Dryer's rule of Antidative) which creates IOs from DOs,
especially those which have some combination of the properties human,
topicalized, partial affectedness, and specificity (1991: 54). Thus an example
such as (18) would contain a direct object at one level of analysis which
would then become an indirect object at a derived level as the result of ADS.
ADS operates under pragmatic conditions similar to those which control
Dative Shift (DS) in languages such as English (e.g. Ransom • 1979, S.
Thompson 1990). However, as Noonan points out, ADS and DS are syntactic
opposites. If DS is seen as a case of "promotion" along a hierarchy of
grammatical relations SUB>DO>IO, then ADS appears to be a case of
OBJECT RELATIONS AND DATIVE CASE IN DOLAKHA NEWARI 53
"demotion". However the semantic and pragmatic properties of the argu-
ments that undergo ADS are those of NPs which typically are given posi-
tions higher on the hierarchy, hence the demotion analysis is counterintuitive.
One could argue instead that in these languages the order of the object
categories in the hierarchy of grammatical relations is reversed, leading to a
SUB>IO>DO pattern. Ideally, evidence for this reversal would be manifest
in a number of different morphosyntactic areas in the grammars of the
languages in question, a situation not found in Dolakha. Note also that Dryer
states that "a language that treats IO's as higher then DO's is really a PO
language" (1986: 819). But, as we have seen, the PO analysis is problematic.
As will be demonstrated below, the only known evidence by which one
could argue for a division of Dolakha objects into DOs and IOs is case-
marking. If there were independent arguments which showed that case-
marked and non-casemarked objects in Dolakha had different morphos-
yntactic behavior, then one would feel justified in saying that there are two
object grammatical relations in Dolakha. Since this is not the case, the
postulation of ADS imposes a grammatical distinction not in evidence in the
language, and only functions to account for the presence of the dative
casemarker on patients. The grammar is simpler if it avoids the postulation
of two levels of structure mediated by syntactic rules, and simply allows
casemarking to be assigned independent of grammatical relations.
3.3 Evidence for a single category "object" in Dolakha
It is clear that in Dolakha there must be at least one grammatical
category of object. That objects are distinct from subjects is shown by the
casemarking facts presented, by the morphology of relative clause construc-
tions (discussed below), and by other criteria which clearly distinguish
subjects as a syntactic category (Genetti 1994). That objects are distinct from
obliques is established by casemarking, and by the morphology of relative
clause constructions. The goal of the arguments given below will therefore
be to illustrate that there are no syntactic rules in this language that distin-
guish between categories of objects; that is, there are no rules which make
a distinction between either DO/IO or PO/SO. This will be done by examin-
ing different types of syntactic behavior which are generally considered to be
relevant to object categories cross-linguistically, specifically word order,
relativization, reflexivization, and causatives.'5 If there were a distinction
between object categories in Dolakha, and if casemarking were correlated

4. Discussion
4.1 The assignment of casemarking in Dolakha
I have shown that four different syntactic structures which commonly
differentiate categories of objects in the languages of the world do not do so
in Dolakha. There is to my knowledge no other syntactic behavior in this
language that could be said to differentiate object categories. The only
possible morphological behavior which could be attributed to such a division
is the presence of the dative casemarker. However using the dative as a
diagnostic is problematic since the dative casemarker is found on patients as
well as on recipients. While it is possible to retain an analysis whereby
casemarking is the result of a division of grammatical relations, for example,
by weakening Dryer's theory of primary objectivity, a much simpler analysis
is obtained by allowing casemarking to be sensitive to semantic and pragmat-
ic, rather than syntactic, factors. This analysis directly reflects the insights
provided by the quantitative study, and does not require dividing Dolakha
objects into two classes for which there is no evidence. The distribution of
the casemarker may thus be summarized as follows:
(33) Distribution of dative casemarking on objects
All recipients
Human patients which are given or accessible
Non-human animate patients which are topical
Note that this system allows for the possibility of two objects marked with
the dative case in ditransitive examples, a situation attested in texts and
exemplified in (8) above. It also allows for a nice parallel for the assignment
of ergative case, which also is not based on grammatical relations. Thus
there is no one-one relation between cases and gramimatical relations for
either ergative or dative case.
When summarized as in (33), however, the distribution of the dative
casemarker, seems to be almost disjunctive. Recipient is a semantic case role
where the presence of the dative case is obligatory (this is true even in the
few cases where the recipient is non-topical or non-referential (though

4.2 Motivation for the casemarking of objects
A number of articles have appeared in the last fifteen years discussing
the interaction of the syntactic relations of objects with semantic and
pragmatic categories (including Comrie 1979, Ransom 1979, Hopper and
Thompson 1980, Mallinson and Blake 1981, Masica 1982, Blansitt 1984,
Givón 1984a, b, Dryer 1986, Foley 1986, S. Thompson 1990, C. Thompson
1990, Croft 1990, La Polla 1992). The papers are remarkably similar in
finding humanness, animacy, and various discourse pragmatic categories
(including definiteness, referentiality, topicality, salience and topicworthiness)
correlating significantly with morphosyntactic alternations evidenced in the
behavior of recipient and patient objects. Thus the current study adds more
evidence to this growing body of literature.
In this literature there are three primary explanations proposed for the
correlations between semantic and pragmatic factors and the morphosyntactic
behavior of objects. Comrie (1979), Masica (1982), Foley (1986) and La
Polla (1992) suggest that marking of animate or definite objects is primarily
caused by a need for disambiguation with agentive NPs. Since subjects of
transitive verbs are overwhelmingly animate and definite (Comrie 1979,
Givón 1976), the propositional semantic structure of clauses in which both
the subject and the object share these features will be more difficult to
recover than in clauses with inanimate or indefinite objects. Thus these
authors claim that the pattern in which languages only mark objects which
are animate and/or definite is motivated by the need for case recoverability.
Dryer (1986: 841) states that the primary motivation for the PO/SO
distinction is discourse/pragmatic function. His argument is as follows. Since
recipients tend to be more topical than patients in ditransitive clauses (see S.
Thompson 1990), they constitute "secondary clausal topics" (in contrast to
agentive NPs which are "primary clausal topics", Givón 1984b). In mono-
transitive clauses, however, the only argument eligible to be a secondary
clausal topic is the patient. He concludes "the PO/SO distinction can be
viewed as a grammaticization of secondary topic versus non-topic". Dryer's
OBJECT RELATIONS AND DATIVE CASE IN DOLAKHA NEWARI 59
discussion does not refer to the role of animacy or particular pragmatic
functions such as definiteness. However in his concluding remarks, Dryer
acknowledges that a human/non-human distinction may be relevant for some
primary object languages. Although none of the languages of his study have
such a system, he speculates that a human/non-human distinction might serve
as a diachronic source for primary objectivity, since human arguments tend
to be topical as well.
Ransom (1979) examines the pragmatic status of patient and recipient
arguments in English double object constructions. She finds that both
definiteness-referentiality and humanness-animacy are relevant factors in
determining the position of recipient arguments in English. She combines the
two parameters into a single "salience hierarchy" and argues that the most
salient of the two arguments will appear first in the clause. Since this study
is restricted to English, the relative word order of the patient and recipient
arguments is the primary formal realization of different pragmatic statuses.
Ransom thus frames the explanation of her results in terms of order, stating
that it is natural to order salient information first.
Of these three explanations, it is clear that neither the proposal by
Ransom based on the ordering of salient information, nor the proposals put
forth independently by Comrie, Masica, Foley and La Polla regarding
disambiguation can be postulated as primary motivation for the pattern in
Dolakha. Ransom's proposal is clearly inadequate for Dolakha since word
order and the appearance of the dative casemarker are not correlated. The
theory that disambiguation is the primary motivation for this pattern is
untenable in the presence of a regular ergative pattern (as noted by Noonan
1991: 56). Since subjects of transitive verbs will always be marked as
ergative, there is no need to use the dative casemarker to disambiguate the
propositional semantics. Thus while disambiguation may be a secondary,
redundant function of the use of dative case on patients, it cannot be the
primary motivation for the pattern.
Dryer's analysis, based on the distinction between primary and second-
ary clausal topics, works well for a language where casemarking follows a
true primary object pattern, but as Noonan (1991: 57) notes, it is not relevant
for languages where only some patients are casemarked. If there are two
arguments in a clause, why should the object NOT become the secondary
clausal topic? In other words, we would expect all patients of mono-
transitives to be casemarked, as in a true PO/SO system.
I believe that the Dolakha pattern is most easily explained by referring

again to the notion of referent importance or topicworthiness. In Dolakha,
animacy and activation cost are the topicality factors which play a role in the
distribution of the dative case. We can arrange these parameters on parallel
hierarchies, alongside a similar hierarchy for case roles (cf. Silverstein 1976,
Dixon 1979, Givón 1984, Comrie 1989, Croft 1990, Chafe 1992):
(34) Recipient > Patient
Human > Animate > Inanimate
Given > Accessible > New
The higher the ranking of an argument on these three hierarchies, the more
likely it is to be casemarked as dative. Since recipients are overwhelmingly
human and given, the presence of the dative case on this category is fully
grammatical. The status of patients, on the other hand, varies significantly,
as patients can hold any combination of features on either the animacy or the
activation cost hierarchy. Only patients with rankings high on these scales
may be casemarked. Note that the lower the patient on the animacy hierar-
chy, the more stringent the pragmatic requirements for dative case become;
non-human animate patients must not only be given, they must also be
playing a significant role in plot resolution to qualify for casemarking. And
inanimates, at the lowest end of the hierarchy, are never casemarked. This
analysis follows Dryer and others in recognizing that it is the topical status
of recipients that allows for the reanalysis of dative casemarkers from a
marker of recipient or indirect object, to a marker of topical objects in
general.
5. Conclusion
This study has explored the factors affecting the distribution of the
assignment of dative case in a language in which dative is extended to some
patients of monotransitive clauses. Animacy and activation cost, factors
which contribute to the overall topicality or "topicworthiness" of an argu-
ment were found to be excellent predictors of the assignment of dative case.
In addition, it has been argued that Dolakha has only one syntactic category
of object, and does not distinguish between either direct and indirect or
primary and secondary objects. Dolakha thus constitutes an additional type
to be added to our typology of object systems, and to be considered in the
development of theories of grammatical relations.

Guidelines for contributors
General: Studies in Language invites contributions in all areas of linguistics,
with special reference for morphology, syntax semantics, pragmatics, historical
linguistics, and discourse. For all contributions, the priority of a typological and
cross-linguistic perspective is high: articles on one language only are welcome if
of interest to the generalist/universalist. Likewise, interdisciplinary studies are
welcome to the exent that they have the same perspective.
Manuscript submission: Manuscripts should be submitted to one of the editors
in triplicate. The print-outs should be double-spaced, keeping wide left and right
margins. For specifics such as typing format, illustrations and tables, references,
glosses, etc., please consult the Sil style sheet (to be found in this issue).
Following receipt of the manuscript on diskette, authors will receive an
acknowledgement of receipt via e-mail.
Editorial policy: Contributions should be in the English language only. Articles
previously published or under consideration by another journal cannot be ac-
cepted. Authors will receive thirty offprints of their articles and fifteen of book
reviews. All correspondence concerning editorial matters should be sent directly
to one of the following editors:
